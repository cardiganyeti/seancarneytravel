A Spiritual Retreat with Turndown Service

Eighteen nights alone on Queen Mary 2, six months from 60, and nowhere left to run.

By Sean Carney · November 2026

I’m six months from 60, and lately I’ve been taking myself apart like a set of Russian nesting dolls. You open one, and there’s another one inside. You open that one, and there’s another. I keep expecting to reach the real me. So far, I keep finding another doll.

The outside one is the man most people know. Paramedic, physician assistant, consultant, project manager, leadership coach, and now travel advisor. Every word of my LinkedIn profile is true. It just isn’t the whole story. A lot of the man in that profile turns out to be habits I learned a long time ago to get by, and somewhere along the way I mistook the habits for me.

Open that one, and there’s regret. I’ve hurt people. Not on purpose, but because I didn’t have my act together, and some days I still don’t. Some of what went wrong in my life was by my own hand and some by the hands of others, and I’ve spent a lot of nights trying to work out the exact split, as if getting the math right would either let me off the hook or finally convict me. It does neither.

Inside regret is time. There’s more road behind me than ahead now. I spent years in emergency medicine, and there were four of us on my old ambulance crew. Two are gone. I’ve known for a long time that nobody is promised a long life. What I didn’t expect was how loud that gets at 59. And here’s the cruel part: worrying about wasted time is one of the most reliable ways I’ve found to waste more of it.

Inside time is the scoreboard. Money, a house, kids, a legacy, some mark on the world that says I was here. Give me any of the usual measures of a successful life, and I promise I haven’t met it. I know it’s the wrong scoreboard. I check it anyway.

And inside all of them is the runner. I’ve reinvented myself so many times that I rarely had to look back. There was always a next idea, a next version of me, a next thing to build. It’s a very efficient way to never stand still long enough to find out who’s under all those hats.

Then, this fall, I ran out of places to run. I’d booked eighteen nights alone on the Queen Mary 2, New York to Southampton and back, and told myself it was a reset, a spiritual retreat with turndown service. What it turned out to be was a ship with no next port, no next project, and nothing asking anything of me for days at a time. So, for once, I looked back. It wasn’t comfortable.

What I found wasn’t clarity. It was something stranger. Every doll I opened turned out to hold two things at once, both completely true. The patterns weren’t my choice, and I’m still responsible for what I did with them. My life looks fine on paper, and it’s far from put together. I’m grateful for the time I have, and I keep wasting it. I’m content on my own, and I’m lonely. Three days from the end of this crossing, I’m more than ready to go home, and I’d book the same round trip again, for the same reason.

For most of my life I’ve believed one side had to be the real one, and that if I could just work out which, I’d finally know who I am and what to do next. I’m starting to think the deciding is the thing that’s been wearing me down.

Here’s something I didn’t know until recently. In a set of nesting dolls, the smallest one is the only one that doesn’t open. It’s solid all the way through. I used to imagine it as the real me, waiting at the center to be found, finally put together. Now I think it’s simpler than that. It’s the part of me that can hold both sides without needing one of them to win.

So is there something to take away from all this? I think there is, and it’s simpler than I expected. Being alright doesn’t mean having it figured out. Some of this can’t be fixed. There’s no way-back machine, and unless I win the Powerball, my next career may well involve a name tag. The regret doesn’t go away, the clock doesn’t slow down, and the scoreboard still doesn’t add up. What isn’t fixed is narrower than I’d like, but it’s real: how I spend the years I’ve got, who I spend them with, and whether I’m actually there for them. Being alright means the despair stops getting the last word. It’s one true thing. It’s never the only one.

On the same morning out here, I can be a man who’s sure he wasted his one life and a man with a good cup of coffee, watching grey light come up over the Atlantic, glad to be here to see it. Both are true. I don’t have to decide which one is the real me before I choose what to do with today. And the second one, it turns out, is reason enough to stick around for tomorrow morning, and the one after that.

I started this crossing joking that I was on a spiritual retreat with turndown service. The joke, it turns out, was on me. Somewhere between New York and Southampton and back again, a man who has spent his whole life running put himself on a ship and, without quite meaning to, held still. Whatever I found out here, I found in spite of myself. It isn’t pretty, but it’s mine, and it’s real.

I haven’t reached the smallest doll yet. But I think I’ll walk off this ship a little closer to it than I was when I walked on.

The ship will be back in New York right on schedule. I’ll get where I’m going when I get there. And I think I’m going to be alright. If you’re in the middle of your own set, I think you are too.
