A Quiet Day in Delft from the Rotterdam Cruise Port

By Sean Carney · November 2026

On a port day, the introvert in me goes looking for the quietest place I can reach by train. Delft is half an hour away, and it was exactly what I was looking for.

Getting there

This is the easy part, and it took me about half an hour from the gangway to the first canal. Here's exactly how, step by step, written by an American who had to figure it out once.

  1. Walk to the Wilhelminaplein metro. The cruise terminal sits on the Wilhelminapier, and the metro station is a walk of about five minutes back along the pier, toward the Erasmus Bridge. Look for the big red-and-white M signs.
  2. Take metro line D or E toward Rotterdam Centraal. Both lines go there, and it's four stops: Leuvehaven, Beurs, Stadhuis, then Rotterdam Centraal, about five minutes. Get off at Centraal even though line E keeps going; it doesn't stop in Delft.
  3. Change to the train. At Rotterdam Centraal you leave the metro through its gates and walk up into the main train station. Look at the departure boards for any train that lists Delft as a stop. Most trains toward Den Haag do, and so do the regular Intercity trains to Amsterdam that go via Den Haag. The one to skip is the Intercity direct to Amsterdam, which runs on the high-speed line and doesn't stop in Delft.
  4. Ride about 10 to 15 minutes to Delft. Trains run several times an hour, so there's no need to plan around a timetable. Delft is a single station, simply called Delft.
  5. Walk into the old town. The station sits at the edge of the center, and the Markt is about a 10- to 15-minute walk.

Paying for it

You don't need to buy a ticket. The Dutch system, called OVpay, lets you tap a contactless Visa or Mastercard, or Apple Pay or Google Pay on your phone or watch, on the card readers at the gates. A few things worth knowing:

  • Tap in and tap out, every time. Tap in at the metro, tap out when you leave it at Centraal, then tap in again at the train gates and out again in Delft. The metro and the trains are run by different companies, so each leg is its own trip.
  • Use the same card or phone for the in and the out. Switching cards partway through looks like two half-finished trips.
  • One card per person. If you're traveling as a couple, you each need your own card or phone.
  • Bring a backup. American Express isn't accepted, and the odd foreign card gets declined. If yours does, the ticket machines at the stations take cards and have an English option.
  • If you forget to tap out, you're charged a higher fare, and for the train it's a lot higher. You can claim the difference back on the OVpay website, but it's easier to remember.

Coming back

Do it in reverse: any train from Delft whose stops include Rotterdam Centraal (the boards will say), then metro line D toward De Akkers or line E toward Slinge, four stops to Wilhelminaplein. Give yourself a comfortable margin before all-aboard; I'd plan on leaving Delft at least an hour and a half before you need to be back on the ship.

What the day is like

Delft is canals, brick, bridges and very little hurry. It's small enough to wander without a map and pretty enough that you don't mind getting a little lost. In November, with the leaves turning and the water still, it was about as peaceful as a town gets.

The Markt, the main square, is the place to start. The town hall sits at one end and the Nieuwe Kerk at the other, and it's worth a slow lap before you head off along the canals.

Walk out to the Oostpoort, the old eastern city gate, with its two pointed towers and its bridge over the water. It's the last of Delft's medieval gates still standing, and it's one of those places that makes you stop and just look for a while.

Coffee, and somewhere to sit

Delft has no shortage of bakeries and coffee places, and that's most of what I wanted from the day. My favorite was Neef Rob, a café that's also a coffee, tea and chocolate shop: warm lamps, deep chairs, and the kind of room where nobody minds if you stay a while. I had a coffee and a slice of appeltaart, the tall Dutch apple pie, and I'd go back for the pie alone.

On your way out, the shop side is a good place to find something to take home that isn't a magnet.

Why it works for a port day

It's close, it's simple to get to on your own, and it asks almost nothing of you. No tour, no tickets to book, no crowds to fight. You can see it in a morning or fill the whole day, and you'll be back at the ship with plenty of time to spare.

Accessibility

Getting there

Most Dutch train stations have a lift or a ramp. If you'd like help getting on and off the train, the Dutch railway, NS, offers free travel assistance. Request it at least an hour before you travel, through the NS app, at ns.nl/reisassistentie or by phone on +31 30 235 7822; the online route needs a free Mijn NS account. Every Rotterdam metro station has lifts, ramps and escalators, according to RET, the metro operator. It's still worth checking RET's service updates the morning you go, in case a lift is out.

Getting around Delft

The town is flat and the distances are short, which helps a lot. The streets are mostly brick, and some are uneven, and many of the canal bridges are gently humped. A café stop every hour or so is easy to arrange, since there's one on nearly every corner.

Would I go back?

In a heartbeat. It was an easy, unhurried, honestly perfect day, the kind I go looking for on every port stop and don't always find. I'm glad I spent it in Delft, and I'd love to come back someday, with the afternoon left open this time.

If a Rotterdam sailing is on your mind, tell me about it.
