Rotterdam, the Netherlands

Arrive Before You Arrive

A night at Rotterdam's Hotel New York, and why the hours on either side of a cruise matter as much as the cruise.

By Sean Carney · November 2026

An itinerary tells you when the ship leaves and when it docks. It has nothing to say about the hours on either side of those two numbers, and those are the hours that decide which version of you boards, and how much of the trip survives the trip home.

I found that out by accident, in a hotel in Rotterdam that used to be an office building.

Getting there from Schiphol

Schiphol is a lot. It's enormous and busy and every sign is competing with four other signs for your attention, and I've never once walked through it feeling like a person in full possession of his faculties. But the part I was braced for turned out to be the easy part. Off the plane, follow the signs down to the trains. They're clear, and in English. I skipped the machines, went to the ticket counter and told the guy where I was headed. He built the whole thing in about ninety seconds: train to Rotterdam Centraal, then the metro south to Wilhelminaplein.

Which I cannot pronounce. And I did not spell it just now either. I copied and pasted it.

Hotel New York

An hour after I picked up my bag, I was standing at the end of a pier in Rotterdam, looking up at the words Holland America Line. Not on a ship. On a building: red brick, two green copper towers, built in 1901, looking like it was dropped in from a different century than everything around it, which in Rotterdam it more or less was. This was the Line's head office. Holland America carried nearly two million emigrants to new lives across the Atlantic, many of them from this same pier, where I was about to board a ship and go look at Scotland for two weeks.

The Line moved its headquarters to the United States in 1977. The building sat empty, then squatters had it, then it was sold on. It reopened as a hotel, Hotel New York, in 1993, and it's the least cookie-cutter place I've stayed in a long time. Old brick building turned into loft-style rooms: wood floors, high ceilings, modern finishes dropped into a hundred-year-old structure. Big bed, big bathroom, Nespresso on the counter. A bathtub sitting out in the room instead of the bathroom, because they upgraded me at check-in. The room on my second stay didn't have it. The air conditioning cleared my bar, which is a higher bar than most people's. The front desk was excellent the whole time, sliding into English without a blink.

Makes sense once you remember what these rooms used to be. They weren't rooms. They were offices.

I'd booked it on purpose. I saw it on a map a few hundred yards from the cruise terminal and thought, good, that solves a problem. What I hadn't understood is that a map and a window are two different pieces of information. The ship isn't a dot on a screen. It's the view. I drank my morning Nespresso looking at the Nieuw Statendam, and that does something to your nervous system a hotel near an airport never does. Nothing left to arrange. Getting aboard asked nothing of me except that I keep putting one foot in front of the other.

A day in Katendrecht

There are rental bikes out front. There are also motorized scooters that come with helmets, which I chose to read as a warning label. I ran the numbers, not on cost but on the odds of spending my evening in a Rotterdam emergency room instead of my morning on that ship, and walked instead.

Across the Rijnhaven pedestrian bridge, ten or fifteen minutes on foot, is Katendrecht. For about a hundred years this was where the port came ashore: sailors' quarter, Chinatown, red-light district. Now it's a small pedestrian square called Deliplein, ringed with terraces. I had coffee at a place called Kopi Soesoe, with an Indonesian angle and the general atmosphere of somebody's very good living room.

There was a market on the square that Saturday, the kind a neighborhood actually shops at: artists, craftspeople, a table of old maps, people buying their food outdoors on a weekend. A stroopwafel trailer pressing them fresh, which is not the thing in the plastic sleeve at the airport. Same name, different food. A fry truck, because it's the Netherlands. And then the strawberries: a carton from a woman at a produce stall, tap the phone, take the fruit, the best strawberries I'd had in years. I wanted them aboard, but had no idea if fresh fruit was allowed on the ship and had to decide standing right there. I hedged with one more carton, and that was it. Nobody on the ship cared even slightly. I should have bought the cherries too.

A few doors off the square is a tattoo shop, one of the last links to when this neighborhood was full of tattoo parlors and sailors got marked before shipping out. It started in a cellar in 1968, and his kids work the counter now. I wanted the T-shirt, not the tattoo, but he doesn't open until midday, and by midday I was on a ship.

Five minutes away is the Fenix, a migration museum in an old harbor warehouse with one subject: people leaving and arriving, and what the crossing does to them. The centerpiece is a labyrinth built from more than two thousand real suitcases, donated from all over the world. I walked through it the day before boarding a ship, a few hundred yards from the pier where so many people left Europe for good.

I could have trained up to Amsterdam instead, done the museums and the canals and come back sweaty and spent. I stayed around one square instead, and I'd make the same call again.

The night after

The next morning I walked to my ship, out the front door and flat the whole way. And I booked the same hotel for the night I got off, which was the deliberate part. On disembarkation morning, a couple of thousand people move toward the same airport in the same hour, and I know what that does to me. Something in my chest goes flat and tactical, and fourteen days of attention are gone in forty minutes of luggage math. So I slept in. No line, no crowd. I dropped my bags, walked back across the bridge, and had breakfast outside while Rotterdam and I both woke up slowly.

That's not a travel hack. It's a buffer. A night on the front end to arrive before I arrive. A morning on the back end to come down on my own clock, in a city I'm in no hurry to leave.

Next time I'll take two.

Accessibility and getting around

Getting to the hotel

From Schiphol it's a train to Rotterdam Centraal and the metro to Wilhelminaplein, then a short walk out along the pier. If stairs, luggage or a wheelchair make that a lot, a taxi or a booked transfer from Schiphol or Rotterdam Centraal takes the guesswork out, and it's one of the first things I'd arrange.

The hotel itself

Hotel New York is a historic building, and the rooms vary a lot from one to the next. Before booking, I'd confirm step-free access to the room you're getting, the bathroom layout, and how far the room is from the lift.

The walk to the ship

The walk from the hotel to the cruise terminal is short and flat. If walking any distance with luggage isn't easy, it's worth asking about help with bags or a short ride.

Katendrecht

The Rijnhaven bridge is for pedestrians, and Deliplein is car-free, so it's an easy, low-key place to spend a few hours without much planning. The walk over is ten to fifteen minutes each way.

Would I book this for a client?

Yes, for anyone sailing from Rotterdam who'd like the trip to start before the ship does. One night before and one after, at least. I'd make it two.
