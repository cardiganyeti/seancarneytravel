Travel well

Why You Come Home Needing a Vacation from Your Vacation

And the planning choices that change it.

By Sean Carney · November 2026

It’s an old joke for a reason. You save for months, you finally go, and you come home more tired than when you left. The trip was great. You’re just wrecked.

Usually it isn’t the destination. It’s how the trip was built. We plan vacations the way we plan work: get the most out of every day, see everything, waste nothing. The itinerary that looks best on paper is often the one that wears you out.

Time has value, just like a dollar, maybe more, and a packed schedule spends it fast. Here’s what I’ve learned, planning my own trips and other people’s, about building a trip that gives you something back.

Give the trip one job

Before you pick a single hotel, decide what the trip is for. Rest? Time with someone you love? Adventure? A big birthday? One word is enough.

For my recent crossing on Queen Mary 2, the word was “reset,” and it made every choice easier, including all the things I didn’t do. When a trip has no job, it tries to do everything. That’s how you end up with three cities in six days and a souvenir magnet from each one.

Count travel days as days

A travel day isn’t a free day with a flight attached. It’s a day. If you land at 3 p.m. after an overnight flight, that evening is gone, and pretending otherwise just means you spend it pretending.

Better yet, arrive before you arrive. Before a sailing out of Rotterdam, I spent the night at the Hotel New York, right by the cruise terminal, and walked onto the ship rested instead of frazzled. A night near the airport or the port costs a little money and buys back a lot of trip.

Stay longer in fewer places

Every time you change hotels, you lose half a day to packing, checking out, getting there and figuring out a new neighborhood. Two bases for a week will almost always feel better than four.

You also start to feel like you live somewhere, even for a few days, and that’s when a place opens up.

Leave white space

Plan one thing a day, maybe two, and leave the rest open. The best moments of most trips aren’t on the itinerary anyway.

On a port day in Rotterdam, I went looking for the quietest place I could reach by train, ended up in Delft, and had an easy, unhurried, honestly perfect day. That only happens if there’s room for it.

Bring a little of home with you

I’m a creature of habit. In any town, I’ll find the coffee shop and the bookstore, and probably a bakery. I used to think that was unadventurous. Now I think it’s how I travel well.

A small daily ritual, like the same morning coffee or an evening walk, gives a trip a rhythm, and a rhythm is what lets you actually relax.

Plan the landing

The trip doesn’t end at the airport. If you land Sunday night and walk into a full Monday, you’ll lose most of what the trip gave you by Tuesday. Give yourself a buffer day at home if you can, or at least a light first day back.

Arrive well, travel more

None of this is about doing less for the sake of it. It’s about coming home with something left in the tank, so you want to go again soon, and you’re well enough to. That’s what I care about when I help someone plan: not the most impressive itinerary, but the one that feels like theirs.
