Should You Cross the Atlantic on Queen Mary 2?

What I’d tell a client after eighteen nights on board.

By Sean Carney · November 2026

Seven days at sea with no land in sight sounds like heaven to some people and a hostage situation to others. I’ve just spent eighteen nights on Queen Mary 2, New York to Southampton and back, and I’m firmly in the first camp.

But a crossing isn’t for everyone, and it’s an expensive way to find out you’re in the second camp. So here’s what I’d tell you if you were sitting across the table from me, trying to decide.

Who a crossing is for

A crossing is for people who think getting there can be the trip. You’ll love it if you like long, unhurried meals, a good book with nowhere to be, and days that don’t need a plan.

It’s also a gift if you hate jet lag. The clocks change an hour at a time, overnight, so you arrive already on local time.

If what you love about travel is a new place every morning, a cruise with ports will probably make you happier. That isn’t a lesser way to travel. It’s just a different trip.

“What do you do all day?”

It’s the question everyone asks, and the honest answer is: as much or as little as you want. There are talks, classes, a planetarium, afternoon tea, live music, and a proper library with thousands of books. There are also deck chairs and a whole lot of ocean.

I found the days filled themselves. Coffee by a window in the morning, a walk on deck, the library, lunch, a book, and a nap, pretty much every day, that I didn’t apologize for.

The first time you realize it’s four in the afternoon and you haven’t looked at your phone, you’ll understand why people come back.

Which direction?

Westbound, toward New York, the clocks go back an hour on most nights, so you get a string of 25-hour days. That’s the easier direction for most people.

Eastbound, toward Southampton, you lose an hour most nights, and some people feel it by midweek. Either way, you skip the jet lag. If you’re sailing one way and flying the other, your dates and how you sleep matter more than any rule of thumb, and it’s worth talking through before you book.

What about rough seas?

Queen Mary 2 is an ocean liner, not a cruise ship. She was built for exactly this crossing, with a deeper hull and a sharper bow than most ships, and she handles the North Atlantic well.

That said, it’s still the North Atlantic. If you’re prone to seasickness, choose a cabin low and toward the middle of the ship, where you’ll feel the least motion, and talk to your doctor before you go.

Window or balcony?

On a port-heavy cruise, a balcony is for watching the world arrive. On a crossing, there’s no land to watch, and on the North Atlantic, especially outside summer, you may not sit out there much.

What you will do is spend real time in your cabin, looking at the sea. A good window, with a chair next to it, may matter more than you’d think.

What to wear

Evenings are dressier than on most cruises, with a few gala evenings on each crossing. If dressing up brings you joy, this is your ship.

If it doesn’t, there are places to eat on gala nights without a tuxedo, and nobody will notice you weren’t in the main dining room. I know this from experience.

Getting there and home

Most people sail one way and fly the other. Queen Mary 2 arrives in Brooklyn early in the morning, and it’s very doable to fly home the same day if you book a later flight.

On the Southampton end, give yourself a night in England before or after, so the trip doesn’t end with a sprint to Heathrow.

Is it worth it?

For me, without question. I’m finishing this crossing more rested than a week at a resort has ever left me. But I’d rather you book the right trip than this trip.
