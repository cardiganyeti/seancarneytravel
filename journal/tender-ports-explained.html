Tender Ports Explained: How to Tell If Your Cruise Has One

By Sean Carney · November 2026

Some ports don't have room for your ship. So the ship drops anchor offshore and small boats run you in. Here's what that day looks like, and how to find out before you book.

What a tender port is

At most ports, the ship ties up alongside a pier and you walk off down a gangway. At a tender port, the ship anchors offshore and smaller boats, called tenders, carry passengers to a landing and back. On many ships the tenders are the ship's own lifeboats doing a second job. In some ports they're local boats.

Ships tender when the harbor is too small or too shallow, when the pier is already taken, or when the ship is simply too big to come alongside. That's why the same port can be docked on one sailing and tendered on another, and why the port's name alone doesn't tell you how your day will work.

What the day looks like

You'll usually get a tender ticket or a time to show up. When it's your turn, you walk down to a platform near the waterline, step across into the boat, and ride in. The landing might be a smooth pier or a set of stone steps. Coming back is the same in reverse, and there's a last tender of the day you'll want to be on.

It all works, but it takes time. On a busy morning, guests on the ship's own excursions often go ashore first, so if you're on your own, the first hour or so may belong to them.

Ports that are often tendered

Santorini, Villefranche (the port for Nice), George Town in Grand Cayman and Cabo San Lucas are well-known examples. Treat any list like this one as a starting point, though. What matters is the plan for your ship on your date.

How to tell if your cruise has one

Start with the cruise line's itinerary page for your exact sailing, not the destination page. Look for words like "tender," "at anchor" or "anchorage." "Docked" or "pier" means the ship plans to tie up.

Then check your booking confirmation. Some lines show it there too. Holland America, for example, notes tender ports in both the voyage itinerary and the booking confirmation.

If it still isn't clear, ask the line:

  • Is this port scheduled to be docked or tendered on this sailing?
  • Is that the same for every ship, or can it vary?
  • Where do passengers board the tender, and how do you get there from the cabin?
  • How will I hear about it if the plan changes?

Accessibility at tender ports

Wheelchairs and scooters

This is where cruise lines differ the most, so it's worth asking about the specific ship. Virgin Voyages, for example, says guests need to manage steps to board most of its tenders, manual wheelchairs need to fold, and motorized wheelchairs and scooters can go only when a roll-on connection between ship and tender is available, which it can't guarantee. Holland America says most of its ships have tender-level elevators that let you roll directly onto the tender platform, though crew can't lift a wheelchair or scooter that weighs more than 100 pounds without its battery.

Steps, gaps and movement

Even on a calm day, there's usually a small gap to cross and a boat that moves a little underfoot. Crew help people across all day long. If balance or steps are a question for anyone in your group, it's one more reason to know the setup ahead of time.

What to ask before you book

  • Does boarding the tender involve steps, and roughly how many?
  • Is there a level or roll-on option at this port, on this sailing?
  • Can this specific chair or scooter go, given its size, weight and battery?
  • Does someone traveling with me need to fold or carry it?
  • What's the landing like: steps, a steep ramp, a long walk to transport?

Don't assume one ship's answer applies to its sister ships. If the answer will decide whether you book, ask the line to put it in writing.

Weather gets a vote

Wind, swell and tides all affect whether tenders can run safely. The captain can delay them, stop them partway through the day, or cancel them. If one port is the whole reason you're taking the cruise, look for a sailing where it's docked, or know the line's policy before you commit. If it's one stop among several, a day on board while most people are ashore isn't the worst consolation prize. You'll rarely see the ship that quiet.

Would I book a cruise with tender ports?

Yes. A tender port is no reason to skip a cruise. When a port matters to you, or tendering is a question for someone in your group, I check how that port works on that ship and that sailing, and you get the line's answer before you commit.
